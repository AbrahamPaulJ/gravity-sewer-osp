1. What an overflow actually is

A gravity sewer is a pipe running downhill, flowing part full. Manholes are vertical shafts down to it, roughly every 40 to 60 metres, and they are the only places a sensor can physically go.

Something blocks the pipe: tree roots, fat, debris. Flow cannot pass. Wastewater backs up behind the blockage, which means upstream of it, because that is the only direction that is uphill. The level rises in the pipe and in the manhole shafts. If it keeps rising it reaches the surface and spills out of a manhole lid, or backs into the lowest household connection. That spill is the overflow.

2. So which sensor sees it

This is the part that is counter-intuitive, and it is the answer to your question.

  • A sensor upstream of the blockage sits in the water that is backing up. Its reading climbs. This is the sensor that detects.
  • A sensor downstream of the blockage is on the dry side. Nothing is reaching it. Its level does not rise; if anything the flow past it drops away.
  • A sensor at the overflow point itself tells you at the moment it is already spilling, which is too late to be worth much.

So detection runs against the flow. A sensor watches the network below it, never above it. That single asymmetry is what makes the problem directional, and it is why a gravity sewer being a directed acyclic graph matters so much: downstream is fixed and never reverses, unlike a pressurised water main.

3. But it only reaches so far, and that is observability

The water backing up does not climb forever. It can only rise until it spills out at the blockage. That gives you a vertical budget: how many metres of height the water can gain before you have an overflow anyway.

The budget is c × manhole depth. Depth is how far it is from the lid down to the pipe invert. The coefficient c = 0.70 is the published value from Ninh et al., and it means we count the blockage as caught only if the sensor gets wet by the time the water is 70 percent up the shaft, leaving 30 percent of freeboard as warning margin.

Now spend that budget. Going upstream, the pipe rises at the gradient. So:

upstream reach = vertical budget ÷ gradient

That is the blue wedge in the diagram. A sensor is inside the wedge or it is not. Inside, it gets wet and detects. Outside, the pipe beneath it is above the water line and it stays dry no matter how bad the blockage gets.

A node is observable if at least one manhole in the network can be inside its wedge. That is the whole definition. Not "is it important", not "is it near a creek". Just: can anywhere upstream get wet before this spills.

4. Why a steep catchment comes out almost blind

Set the presets against each other. The steep catchment has a median gradient of 1.64 percent, which is steep, and the sandbox has no manhole depths to work with so it substitutes the pipe diameter, 0.15 m. Budget becomes 0.105 m, reach becomes about 6 m, and manholes are 53 m apart. The wedge does not reach the next manhole. Nothing sees anything, which is exactly why the sandbox reports only 394 observable nodes out of 3,377.

Now switch to real manhole depth. At 2.5 m the budget is 1.75 m, the reach is about 107 m, and two manholes upstream are covered. On a flatter catchment the same depth reaches five.

This is the finding worth carrying forward. "Two up, two down" is not folklore. At realistic manhole depths on this terrain the geometry lands on almost exactly two manholes upstream, so the rule of thumb encodes real physics. What breaks it is not the rule, it is that manhole depth is not published anywhere, so the model is running on pipe diameter and understating the reach by a factor of roughly fifteen. Getting surveyed manhole depths is worth more to this project than any algorithm change.

5. How the code writes this down

Same geometry, rearranged. For a candidate sensor at node s and a blockage at a downstream node v, water at v rises to elevation elev[v] + c*depth[v], and it reaches the sensor if that is above the pipe invert at s:

elev[v] + c * depth[v] > elev[s]

Read left to right it is the water level at the blockage. Read right to left it is the pipe bottom at the sensor. The comparison is just "does the water get up there".

Three things to keep in mind when you read osp_core.js:

  1. computeObservable loops over sensors and collects descendants, so it answers "what can this sensor see". The diagram above answers the mirror question, "who can see this blockage". Same condition, read from the other end.
  2. Strict descendants only, so a node never observes itself. A sensor in the same manhole as the blockage is not credited, which is conservative.
  3. The real network is not one tidy line. Elevation at a node is the lowest invert recorded there, so it does not fall monotonically downstream, which is why the code walks every descendant instead of pruning on elevation. That prune was tried and it silently dropped real coverage.

6. What this diagram simplifies

  • One straight line at constant gradient and constant manhole depth. Real branches join, gradients change, ground undulates.
  • The backed-up surface is drawn flat. A real backwater profile curves slightly.
  • "Wet" is treated as "detected". A real sensor needs a rise it can distinguish from normal flow, so the true reach is a little shorter than the geometry suggests.
  • Nothing here is calibrated against a South Australian blockage. The 0.70 came from someone else's network.
