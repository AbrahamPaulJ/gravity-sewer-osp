So is a manhole just an open pipe end?

No, and the difference matters. The sewer is a closed pipe between chambers. At each chamber the pipe stops, discharges into an open half channel cast into the concrete floor, and a new pipe carries the flow onward. Around that channel the floor is built up into sloped shoulders called benching, so anything that spills out of the channel drains back into it rather than sitting there.

Above the channel is a shaft you could stand in, typically 1.05 to 1.2 m across, made of stacked precast concrete rings, with step irons up the back wall. It is capped by a cast iron frame and cover at road level.

So the useful mental model is: a pipeline with a small room every 50 metres or so. The rooms exist for access, because a jetting or rodding crew has to be able to reach every length of pipe from one end or the other, and one is placed at every junction, every change of direction, gradient or diameter.

Why that structure is what makes sensing possible at all

Two consequences fall straight out of it, and the whole placement model rests on them.

  1. The chambers are the only openings. Between them the sewer is sealed, so when it backs up the pipe runs completely full and the water keeps climbing up the shafts. The shaft acts as a standpipe. The height it reaches in each shaft is the hydraulic grade line, which is why the flat blue surface in the other explainer is a fair approximation.
  2. The chambers are the only places a sensor can go. You need a lid to open, a dry place to bolt a device and an air gap to measure across. That is why the candidate set in the sandbox is manholes and not "anywhere on the pipe". It is a physical constraint, not a modelling convenience.

Slide the flow depth up. In normal dry weather the flow is a shallow stream in the bottom of the channel, and the sensor under the lid is measuring a long way down to it. As the pipe surcharges, the water leaves the channel, fills the pipe and rises into the shaft. The sensor sees the gap close. That closing gap is the entire signal.

What the sensor is, physically

The level sensor is usually an ultrasonic or radar head bolted under the cover, firing straight down and timing the echo off the water surface. It measures the air gap, and depth of flow is the mounting height minus that gap. Nothing gets wet in normal operation, which is why these can be installed without entering the chamber. The cheaper float switch is a different thing entirely: a float on an arm at one fixed height, which tips and reports a single bit when the water reaches it. It cannot tell you the level, only that it went past. That is the 5 to 6 times cost difference in the project scope.

Question 2: why is every manhole a different depth?

The first tab draws one chamber and lets you set its depth with a slider, which quietly implies depth is something a chamber has. It is not. A chamber is just the vertical distance between two lines that were drawn for unrelated reasons:

  • the ground surface, which falls at whatever the street happens to fall at, and
  • the pipe, which falls at whatever gradient keeps sewage moving fast enough not to drop its solids — roughly 1% for a 150 mm main, and not negotiable.

Nobody reconciles the two. Set the ground slope below the pipe gradient and the run buries itself deeper with every reach; set it above and the pipe climbs toward the surface until the designer has to give up and start again with a drop structure. Drag the two sliders against each other and watch the dimension lines on each chamber.

Why this is the reason the sandbox exists in the shape it does

Chamber depth is the single most important number in the placement model, because the water backing up behind a blockage has to rise within the chamber far enough for a sensor to tell it apart from a normal day. A two metre chamber gives you two metres of headroom to detect in. A one metre chamber gives you one, and it spills sooner.

And depth is published almost nowhere. Because it drifts along every run for the reason above, there is no single figure that stands in for a network: in the demonstration data it runs from about 1.9 m at the tenth percentile to 3.9 m at the ninetieth, in one small suburb. That is why the tool reconstructs it as cover level minus invert level for every chamber, and why it reports the error of that reconstruction rather than asserting a number.

Spacing, and what it costs you

Chambers exist so that a person and a jetting hose can reach the pipe, so their spacing is set by hose length and by the rule that you must be able to see from one to the next, not by anything to do with sensing. Typical runs sit around 50 to 80 m.

That spacing is also the resolution limit of a level-sensor network. Published work on identifying blockages from level alone finds detection degrading with distance from the obstruction and recommends spacing no more than about 100 m if you want to locate the problem rather than merely notice it. Push the spacing slider past that and the run still works as a sewer; it stops working as an instrument.

Question 3: what happens when several pipes arrive at once?

The first two tabs assume one pipe in and one out, which is most chambers but not all. In the demonstration network 136 of 1,010 chambers take two incoming pipes and 21 take three. This is a detail view of one of them, drawn at true proportions with the soil taken away, because at the squashed scale of a street run the branches would be shorter than the chamber is wide.

Why a junction is the first place you would put a sensor

Everything arriving down any of these pipes passes through this one chamber. A sensor here covers several upstream lines at once, for the price of one installation, one battery and one site visit. That is a genuinely better deal than the same sensor on a plain through chamber, and it is the intuition behind every centrality-based placement heuristic in the sandbox: chambers that many paths run through are worth more.

What it costs you in return

A rise here says something is wrong upstream. On a through chamber that is one direction. Here it is two or three, and the sensor cannot tell you which. You have bought coverage and spent precision. Whether that is the right trade depends on whether the job is to notice a problem or to find it — and the honest answer is that a level sensor was always better at noticing.

Do the inlets sit above the outlet, and is the outlet bigger?

Both are reasonable expectations and both were worth checking rather than assuming, so they were tested against every pipe in the demonstration network, reading each pipe's own upstream and downstream invert and nominal diameter from the publisher's mains layer.

So the answer splits. A through chamber is usually built level, which is why the through pipe here is drawn that way. At a junction it is close to a coin toss: 46% of arriving pipes come in above the outlet, by a median of nothing but a ninetieth percentile of 153 mm. The default branch soffit on the slider sits inside that range.

On diameter the expectation does not hold. The pipe out is the same nominal size as the largest pipe in at 96.9% of chambers, larger at only 2.7%, and smaller at 0.4% — three records that all look like errors. A sewer steps up a size at intervals along a trunk, not at every chamber, so drawing the outlet the same size is the honest default.

Switch the outlet to one size up and watch the pipes in rise. Sizes are matched at the soffit, not the invert: the tops align, so a larger pipe out has to be met by pipes arriving higher above its invert. That is one of the two mechanisms that puts an inlet above an outlet, and it is forced by geometry rather than chosen. The slider is the other: whatever further fall the designer allowed across the chamber.

The outgoing invert is the datum throughout. It is the floor of the chamber, the lowest thing in it, and the level everything drains to, so it is what the depth is measured to and what the arriving pipes are set above. Nothing here moves it.

Why the measurement literature cares about this

Alshami et al. (2023) instrumented twelve Hong Kong manholes for a year and recorded, for each, the cover level, the invert level, and the diameter and invert of every inlet and outlet pipe. Ten of the twelve had two or three inlets. Their finding is that the standard reading of a flow sensor — that it is valid while the sewage level stays within the diameter of the outlet pipe — "only holds true for manholes with one inlet and outlet pipe and reasonable flow velocities", and that manholes with multiple inlets show "increased inconsistency in flow direction", sometimes reporting negative velocity.

Which is the sharper version of this tab's point. A junction is the best place to put a sensor by coverage and one of the worst by signal quality, and nothing in the topology tells you that. Note also what is not in the library: no paper here discusses soffit or obvert matching at all, so the convention above is drawn from the data rather than cited from the corpus.

And it changes where a spill can come out

Look at where the branches meet the chamber. They arrive above the through invert, benched down into the main channel so their flow joins without ponding — a branch laid lower than the main it joins would simply back up.

It is tempting to read that as a cap on how high the water can climb. It is not. Water backing up behind a blockage keeps rising past every soffit it meets; what changes at the branch soffit is the set of places the water can reach. Above that line the branch is flooded too, so every opening along it — another chamber lid, a property relief gully in someone's back garden — becomes a candidate escape point.

Drag the soffit slider and watch the dashed ring move. A blockage below this chamber can therefore surface on a street nobody was watching, several branches away from the pipe that actually caused it.

This is exactly why the sandbox does not read a level off one chamber. It floods the whole region upstream of the blockage and takes the lowest opening anywhere in it, which is the only way to get the answer right when the flooded region keeps growing sideways at every junction.

Question 3: does the overflow have to be at a manhole?

No, and in Australia it usually is not.

When the sewer surcharges, water rises to the same level everywhere in the backed-up reach, because it is one connected body of water. It then escapes at whichever opening is lowest. The candidates are:

  • A property overflow relief gully, the ORG. This is a grated drain in the yard of every connected property, deliberately set below the lowest fixture inside the house. Its entire purpose is to be the lowest escape, so that a surcharge comes up in the garden and not through someone's shower. In a normal residential street this is the first thing to spill.
  • A manhole lid, if a chamber further downhill has a lower cover level than any ORG in the reach. Then it lifts and spills into the road.
  • A house fixture, if the ORG is blocked, paved over or built up. This is the expensive one: sewage inside the property.
  • An engineered relief point discharging to a creek, which is the EPA-reportable case.

Switch the blockage between the two positions and watch the spill point change. It is almost never at the blockage. The blockage is the cause; the spill happens wherever the ground happens to be lowest in the flooded reach, which can be a hundred metres away and on someone's property.

What that means for the model

The condition in osp_core.js uses c * depth[v] as the height the water can gain before spilling, measured at the blockage node. That is a proxy. The true ceiling is the lowest escape elevation anywhere in the reach, which needs cover levels and ORG levels we do not have. Since the ground generally falls downhill and the reach extends upstream, the lowest escape is usually near the blockage, so the proxy is defensible. The coefficient c = 0.70 rather than 1.00 is doing exactly this job: acknowledging that something spills a bit before the shaft is full.

Question 4: what if the blockage is at a manhole?

Then yes, the sensor in that chamber is standing in the water and detects it immediately. Set the blockage to "At chamber B" and you can see it: both sensors go wet.

Three things worth separating out.

  1. It is the uncommon case. A chamber is a room; the pipe is a 150 to 300 mm hole. Roots enter at pipe joints and at lateral connections, and fat and rag build up in the barrel or at the pipe's entry to the chamber. The wide part is the least likely place to choke completely.
  2. It is uninteresting as a placement question. "Put the sensor exactly where the problem is" is not a strategy, it is the absence of one. There are 3,377 manholes in a real catchment and a budget for perhaps forty sensors. What decides a placement is not the blockage you happen to be standing on, it is how far each sensor's influence reaches to cover the ones you did not instrument.
  3. The code deliberately does not credit it. There is a test asserting no node observes itself, so a sensor at v is never counted as observing v. This slightly understates every sensor's value, but it does so uniformly across all seven algorithms, so the comparison stays fair. It is the conservative choice.

The genuinely interesting asymmetry is the one in the other direction. Look at what happens with the blockage in the pipe barrel: chamber A upstream goes wet and detects, chamber B downstream stays dry. Same pipe, two chambers, one signal. Detection only ever runs backwards up the network.

Which is the honest gap in the current model. Chamber B is not actually blind here. It is downstream of a blockage, so the flow arriving at it drops away to nothing, and a level sensor would read that as an unexplained dry spell. That is a real detection signal and the model scores it as zero. It is also most of what the "two down" half of the rule of thumb is for. Adding a starved-flow term is the most defensible extension to the objective function, and it would let the sandbox score that rule of thumb on both of the things the rule is actually doing.
