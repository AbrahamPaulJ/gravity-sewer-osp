So is a manhole just an open pipe end?

No, and the difference matters. The sewer is a closed pipe between chambers. At each chamber the pipe stops, discharges into an open half channel cast into the concrete floor, and a new pipe carries the flow onward. Around that channel the floor is built up into sloped shoulders called benching, so anything that spills out of the channel drains back into it rather than sitting there.

Above the channel is a shaft you could stand in, typically 1.05 to 1.2 m across, made of stacked precast concrete rings, with step irons up the back wall. It is capped by a cast iron frame and cover at road level.

So the useful mental model is: a pipeline with a small room every 50 metres or so. The rooms exist for access, because a jetting or rodding crew has to be able to reach every length of pipe from one end or the other, and one is placed at every junction, every change of direction, gradient or diameter.

Why that structure is what makes sensing possible at all

Two consequences fall straight out of it, and the whole placement model rests on them.

  1. The chambers are the only openings. Between them the sewer is sealed, so when it backs up the pipe runs completely full and the water keeps climbing up the shafts. The shaft acts as a standpipe. The height it reaches in each shaft is the hydraulic grade line, which is why the flat blue surface in the other explainer is a fair approximation.
  2. The chambers are the only places a sensor can go. You need a lid to open, a dry place to bolt a device and an air gap to measure across. That is why the candidate set in the sandbox is manholes and not "anywhere on the pipe". It is a physical constraint, not a modelling convenience.

Slide the flow depth up. In normal dry weather the flow is a shallow stream in the bottom of the channel, and the sensor under the lid is measuring a long way down to it. As the pipe surcharges, the water leaves the channel, fills the pipe and rises into the shaft. The sensor sees the gap close. That closing gap is the entire signal.

What the sensor is, physically

The level sensor is usually an ultrasonic or radar head bolted under the cover, firing straight down and timing the echo off the water surface. It measures the air gap, and depth of flow is the mounting height minus that gap. Nothing gets wet in normal operation, which is why these can be installed without entering the chamber. The cheaper float switch is a different thing entirely: a float on an arm at one fixed height, which tips and reports a single bit when the water reaches it. It cannot tell you the level, only that it went past. That is the 5 to 6 times cost difference in the project scope.

Question 2: does the overflow have to be at a manhole?

No, and in Australia it usually is not.

When the sewer surcharges, water rises to the same level everywhere in the backed-up reach, because it is one connected body of water. It then escapes at whichever opening is lowest. The candidates are:

  • A property overflow relief gully, the ORG. This is a grated drain in the yard of every connected property, deliberately set below the lowest fixture inside the house. Its entire purpose is to be the lowest escape, so that a surcharge comes up in the garden and not through someone's shower. In a normal residential street this is the first thing to spill.
  • A manhole lid, if a chamber further downhill has a lower cover level than any ORG in the reach. Then it lifts and spills into the road.
  • A house fixture, if the ORG is blocked, paved over or built up. This is the expensive one: sewage inside the property.
  • An engineered relief point discharging to a creek, which is the EPA-reportable case.

Switch the blockage between the two positions and watch the spill point change. It is almost never at the blockage. The blockage is the cause; the spill happens wherever the ground happens to be lowest in the flooded reach, which can be a hundred metres away and on someone's property.

What that means for the model

The condition in osp_core.js uses c * depth[v] as the height the water can gain before spilling, measured at the blockage node. That is a proxy. The true ceiling is the lowest escape elevation anywhere in the reach, which needs cover levels and ORG levels we do not have. Since the ground generally falls downhill and the reach extends upstream, the lowest escape is usually near the blockage, so the proxy is defensible. The coefficient c = 0.70 rather than 1.00 is doing exactly this job: acknowledging that something spills a bit before the shaft is full.

Question 3: what if the blockage is at a manhole?

Then yes, the sensor in that chamber is standing in the water and detects it immediately. Set the blockage to "At chamber B" and you can see it: both sensors go wet.

Three things worth separating out.

  1. It is the uncommon case. A chamber is a room; the pipe is a 150 to 300 mm hole. Roots enter at pipe joints and at lateral connections, and fat and rag build up in the barrel or at the pipe's entry to the chamber. The wide part is the least likely place to choke completely.
  2. It is uninteresting as a placement question. "Put the sensor exactly where the problem is" is not a strategy, it is the absence of one. There are 3,377 manholes in a real catchment and a budget for perhaps forty sensors. What decides a placement is not the blockage you happen to be standing on, it is how far each sensor's influence reaches to cover the ones you did not instrument.
  3. The code deliberately does not credit it. There is a test asserting no node observes itself, so a sensor at v is never counted as observing v. This slightly understates every sensor's value, but it does so uniformly across all seven algorithms, so the comparison stays fair. It is the conservative choice.

The genuinely interesting asymmetry is the one in the other direction. Look at what happens with the blockage in the pipe barrel: chamber A upstream goes wet and detects, chamber B downstream stays dry. Same pipe, two chambers, one signal. Detection only ever runs backwards up the network.

Which is the honest gap in the current model. Chamber B is not actually blind here. It is downstream of a blockage, so the flow arriving at it drops away to nothing, and a level sensor would read that as an unexplained dry spell. That is a real detection signal and the model scores it as zero. It is also most of what the "two down" half of the rule is for. Adding a starved-flow term is the most defensible extension to the objective function, and it would let the sandbox score the two up, two down rule of thumb on both of the things the rule is actually doing.
